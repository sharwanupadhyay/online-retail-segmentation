We selected the problem of identifying patterns in global e-commerce sales. Our aim is to turn transaction records into useful customer actions. We use the supplied Online Retail II workbook, containing 1,067,371 rows from 2009 to 2011. In this demonstration, we explain how we cleaned the records, what we learned about sales, how we grouped customers, and how the saved model handles a new example. We focus on evidence that could guide a practical business test. Our central question is which different actions are supported by different purchasing histories. We keep the assumptions visible so a reviewer can trace each conclusion back to the transactions.

We removed 34,335 exact duplicates, then excluded 5,581 original non-product rows and 237 additional fee, sample and voucher rows. Missing customer IDs excluded 226,761 otherwise valid purchase rows worth 2,575,278.90 pounds. We did not guess those identities. We matched cancellations only to earlier purchases by the same customer for the same product, starting with the most recent purchase. Of the retained cancellation rows, 15,872 were fully matched, 127 partly matched, and 1,915 unmatched. Reversals reduced identified merchandise value by 730,004.12 pounds, leaving 16,338,578.60 pounds. Unmatched cancelled units remain separately reported.

Our exploratory findings show why definitions matter. Complete fourth-quarter months averaged 1.53 times the revenue of other months. We marked the incomplete final month instead of treating it as a complete sales decline. Regency Cakestand 3 Tier led product revenue at 261,233 pounds. World War 2 Gliders Asstd Designs led units with 103,697, while White Hanging Heart T-Light Holder reached 1,484 customers. EIRE led export revenue from only 3 customers. Netherlands averaged 2,538.50 pounds per order. Large baskets and small customer bases deserve investigation, but do not prove buyer type.

Our clustering inputs are Recency, Frequency, Monetary. These measure days since a purchase, distinct purchase invoices, and net spending. We use K-Means to group similar purchasing histories. We also profile AOV, meaning average order value, Variety, meaning product breadth, and CancellationRate, meaning the proportion of invoices that are cancellations. A logarithm reduces scale differences before standardisation. We compare basic and extended features across candidate cluster counts and check agreement across 10 starting seeds. We select the model using the same evaluation rule for every feature set and candidate. Highest silhouette selected 2 clusters. The silhouette is 0.438; the groups overlap, and their business usefulness still needs testing. We retain exceptional spenders separately using a fence calculated from the spending distribution.

Loyal champions has 2,347 customers and 60.35 percent of net revenue. Offer early access to relevant products and test a replenishment reminder against a holdout group. Dormant occasional buyers has 3,452 customers and 12.10 percent of net revenue. Test a low-cost reminder using their last product category before committing a wider recovery budget. Outlier-Wholesale has 40 customers and 27.54 percent of net revenue. Review major accounts individually, confirm buyer needs and set stock reservations against their observed repeat orders. These actions differ because the profiles differ. The outlier group label does not establish that the buyers are wholesalers. Each proposed action still needs a controlled business test before wider use.

We reload the saved pipeline to test a made-up customer. The inputs are 96 days of recency, 3 purchase invoices, and 837 pounds of net spending. Average order value is consistently 279 pounds. Product variety is 44, and the cancellation ratio is 0. The pipeline uses its saved feature order and predicts Dormant occasional buyers. This demonstrates that the saved artifact works; it does not measure future prediction accuracy.

Our limitations shape our recommendation. Missing identities exclude 13.11 percent of valid gross purchase value, while 3.78 percent of cancelled units cannot be linked. We have no browsing behaviour, marketing spend, margins or return reasons. This is a single historical retailer, and the groups describe past purchasing rather than causes. We recommend focused retention tests and individual review of major accounts, alongside better identity and return records. We would compare treated customers with an untreated group and measure net purchasing after costs. We used AI to assist coding, drafting and checks, with numerical claims verified by execution.
